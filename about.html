Why Standard Models Travel Poorly

Much of contemporary macroeconomics assumes that domestic marginal cost anchors inflation, since the import share of GDP is often below 30 percent; that fiscal dominance is absent, so monetary dominance gives policy strong traction through expectations and interest rates; and that nominal exchange rates are near-random-walks, so they mainly absorb shocks.

In many developing economies, however, the nominal exchange rate is trend depreciating and behaves more like a shock transmitter than a shock absorber. High import shares, often above 30 percent of GDP, limit expenditure switching, while dominant currency pricing weakens the expansionary effects of depreciation. Under these conditions, monetary policy has weaker real effects, and economic overheating, fiscal sustainability, and financial stability cannot be understood adequately through standard macro tools alone.

New Macro Concepts

Overheating on Ice

Economic overheating can emerge through foreign-exchange pressure before it appears in conventional CPI inflation.

Composite Output Gap

A measure of slack that combines domestic and external constraints on economic activity.

Open-Economy Golden Rule

Public investment must satisfy both productive and foreign-exchange constraints.

Missing-Imports Problem

Resource-spending models can misdiagnose adjustment when the imported supply response is omitted.

Monetary Origins of Dutch Disease

Dutch disease can arise from monetary financing and fiscal dominance rather than resource spending alone.

Stock-Flow-Consistent Fiscal Rule

Identifies the primary balance required for debt stability while remaining consistent with goods market equilibrium at potential output.
