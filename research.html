Fiscal Reporting under Fiscal Dominance: A Decomposition of Net Domestic Financing

· Substack · Last updated: June 2026
This article examines a fiscal-reporting problem that arises under fiscal dominance: net domestic financing may be recorded as domestic borrowing even when its economic substance reflects central-bank financing of the fiscal deficit. Under monetary dominance, domestic borrowing replaces private demand with public demand, so accounting classification and economic substance coincide. This article uses a financial transactions matrix to show why conventional fiscal accounting travels poorly to fiscal-dominance settings. It distinguishes demand-absorbing domestic borrowing from direct central-bank financing and securitised monetary financing, where commercial banks swap non-borrowed and non-interest-bearing excess reserves for government securities. An application to Guyana shows that banking-system financing in the 2025 fiscal accounts is explained by direct and securitised central-bank financing. The article recommends a more detailed reporting format to improve fiscal transparency, economic diagnosis, and public accountability.

Dutch Disease as a Money Shock: The Missing-Imports Problem

· Substack · Last updated: May 2026
This paper develops a model in which the composition of fiscal financing determines whether resource booms generate Dutch disease effects. Resource-financed spending can be absorbed through imports, while money-financed spending raises domestic demand without expanding import capacity. The result is real exchange-rate pressure and contraction in the tradable sector. The paper shows that identifying Dutch disease empirically requires attention to monetary diagnostics.

The Open-Economy Golden Rule of Public Finance

· Substack · Last updated: October 2026
This article derives the open-economy Golden Rule of public finance. While the conventional condition equates the net marginal product of public capital with the world interest rate, the open-economy rule adjusts both the marginal return and the marginal cost of capital for its foreign exchange (FX) footprint. Efficiency requires the cost-normalised FX-adjusted return to equal the world interest rate.
